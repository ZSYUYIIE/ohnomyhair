# Swap three round numbers for US$17,200

A realistic, defensible 12-week budget to polish and release "Oh No My Hair" on itch.io and Steam is **about US$17,200 in cash**. That covers both founders at US$1,600 a month each, freelance art, music and sound effects, the Steam fee, remote playtests, tools, store art, a budget trailer and a 20% contingency. A **lean, self-funded version costs about US$900**, and a **stretch version about US$26,500**. The pitch's US$2,000 per person per month is defensible: it sits inside Rami Ismail's widely cited US$1,600–2,200 default for small indie teams. What makes the pitch look fake is that it shows none of its working. It rounds 12 weeks up to 3 months, leaves out every release cost (Steam fee, store art, trailer), carries a contingency bigger than its whole art-and-audio line, and adds US$60,000 and US$120,000 tiers with no scope and no funder. Itemised at the pitch's own US$2,000 rate, the budget comes to about US$19,800, so the original total was roughly right. It just wasn't shown. Funding is thin. For a two-person Godot team the realistic sources are the founders' own money, a Kickstarter campaign (if one founder lives in a supported country) and festival prizes, which are upside only. WINGS finishing funds fit the size and timing exactly, but only for teams founded or co-founded by women or gender-marginalised developers. Epic MegaGrants requires Unreal, and the indie funds start at about US$50,000. At the recommended stipend the founders still put in between about **US$3,300 and US$46,000 of unpaid time**, depending on the benchmark. That belongs on the budget as their own stake, separate from the cash ask.

All figures are in US dollars. Twelve weeks is treated as **2.76 months** (84 days ÷ 30.44). The few prize amounts quoted in euros or won were converted at assumed rates (US$1.15/€, ₩1,400/US$), not sourced ones. No country-specific grants, university programmes or business-registration fees are included, because they depend on where the founders live.

## US$2,000 a month is defensible, but the rest of the pitch is unshown

The largest line in the pitch holds up. Rami Ismail, whose budgeting guide is the most-cited reference for small teams, writes that "for smaller projects with younger developers" he uses **US$1,600–2,200 of monthly salary** as a default. He calls US$2,200 a "globally average" salary for independent developers who own part of their company ([LTPF: Budget Viability](https://ltpf.ramiismail.com/budget-viability)). US$2,000 per person per month falls inside that range, and it is about one-fifth of the **US$10,000 per person per full-time month** that publishers budget, according to Greg Lobanov ([SteamPageAnalyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/indie-game-publisher-vs-self-publishing)). It is also far below US salaried pay. The GDC 2025 salary survey put the US median at about **US$129,000 a year** ([GDC](https://gdconf.com/article/us-game-development-salaries-in-2025-what-our-latest-industry-report-reveals/)). Ismail himself warns that the default "can either be ridiculously high or not nearly enough money" depending on where a team lives, which is the main thing a reviewer might push back on.

The two larger tiers do not survive a check. At the pitch's own US$2,000 rate, two founders cost about **US$22,080 over 24 weeks (37% of US$60,000)** and about **US$33,120 over 36 weeks (28% of US$120,000)**. The pitch never says what the remaining US$38,000 and US$87,000 would buy. The prototype already has a fixed scope of four chapters, twelve rooms and a three-phase boss, so a reviewer will read the extra money as unexplained. There is a legitimate version of a bigger tier, but it describes a different project. Publishers budget about **US$50,000 per platform for a console port** and about US$2,500 per language to localise a 25,000-word game ([SteamPageAnalyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/indie-game-publisher-vs-self-publishing)). "Steam release plus one console port" would land near US$60,000, but it would have to be pitched to a fund or publisher with its own schedule, not mentioned in passing in a 12-week video.

The word "incubation" has the same problem. None of the international sources researched for this report offers incubation, in the startup-accelerator sense, to a two-person Godot game team. The money that does exist comes in specific forms. Indie funds invest for a share of revenue: Outersloth takes 50% of net revenue until recouped and 15% after ([gamedev.net](https://gamedev.net/news/2054-outersloth-reveals-its-game-funding-agreement-with-developers/)), and WINGS takes 50/50 until recouped once and then 15% ([WINGS FAQ](https://www.wingsfund.me/faq)). Publishers pay advances, crowdfunding brings in backers, and festivals award prizes. Asking for "incubation" tells a reviewer the team hasn't decided which of these it wants. Naming the actual asks, such as backers, playtesters or a publishing partner, gives the reviewer something concrete to say yes to.

Small releases really do cost this little. The solo game *Mythscroll* shipped on Steam for **US$246** in total: the Steam fee, US$130 of capsule art and a few dollars of asset packs ([r/IndieDev postmortem](https://www.reddit.com/r/IndieDev/comments/1mtmggy/postmortem_my_first_game_with_a_total_budget_of)). *iZBOT* spent about **US$1,000** on art, ads, licences and fees ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/izbot-indie-game-postmortem)). A finished, publicly playable prototype needs polish money, not production money, so a large share of any budget beyond the lean tier is the founders' pay.

## Three tiers from US$900 to US$26,500 ship the same game

Every tier funds the same deliverable: the existing 4-chapter, 12-room game, polished and released on itch.io and Steam for Windows within 12 weeks. The engine and most tools cost nothing. Godot is MIT-licensed for commercial use ([Godot](https://godotengine.org/license/)), Krita is free for commercial work ([Krita](https://krita.org/en/about/license)), and GitHub Free includes 2,000 Actions minutes a month and 10 GiB of LFS storage ([GitHub Actions](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/concepts/product-billing/github-actions); [GitHub LFS](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/concepts/product-billing/git-lfs)). itch.io charges nothing upfront and takes a seller-chosen revenue share that defaults to 10% ([itch.io](https://itch.io/docs/creators/payments)). A Mac build is left out of every tier because it needs the US$99-a-year Apple Developer Program and access to a Mac ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/programs/)).

The tiers differ in three ways: whether the founders are paid, how much art and audio is commissioned rather than taken from libraries, and how much goes on the Steam storefront. Contingency is **30% in the lean tier**, following Ismail's margin, because its cash lines are tiny and easy to blow. It is **20% in the recommended tier**, GameDevProducer's floor and the top of the 10–20% publishers advise for new teams. It is **15% in the stretch tier**, where most of the money sits in fixed stipends and quoted commissions. Playtesting is budgeted through Antidote. Its free Indie plan runs one playtest a month with up to 20 players for teams under 10 people with no publisher, and pay-as-you-go players start at US$25 each ([Antidote Pricing](https://antidote.gg/pricing/); [Antidote FAQ](https://antidote.gg/faq)). Whether Antidote supports two players on one machine is unconfirmed, so the team should plan for some in-person couch tests as well.

Without stipends, the recommended tier's production and release costs come to **US$5,485, or about US$6,580 with their share of contingency**. That split matters for funding, because the non-stipend part is small enough for founders to cover themselves. Two schedule rules apply to every tier. Steam requires a Coming Soon page to be live for at least two weeks before release, and the page has to go to review at least seven business days before that ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/coming_soon)). The store page therefore has to be submitted by **about week 8 or 9**. Valve also withholds **30% of US-source revenue** from developers in countries without a US tax treaty ([Steamworks Taxes FAQ](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/finance/taxfaq)). That leaves the cost budget unchanged but should temper any revenue claims, especially since SteamPageAnalyzer's model puts the median Steam game's lifetime gross at about **US$1,136** ([SteamPageAnalyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/how-much-do-indie-game-developers-make)).

## The founders still give up between about US$3,300 and US$46,000 of time

The recommended stipend is below every benchmark except Ismail's floor, so the difference should appear on the budget as the founders' in-kind contribution, separate from the cash ask. Three benchmarks bracket it. The low one is Ismail's US$2,200 "globally average" indie salary. The middle one is SteamPageAnalyzer's "modest" US$40,000 a year per founder, about US$3,333 a month ([SteamPageAnalyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/how-much-do-indie-game-developers-make)). The high one is the publisher budgeting rate of US$10,000 per person-month ([SteamPageAnalyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/indie-game-publisher-vs-self-publishing)), which UMBC's production course puts at US$10,000–15,000 once taxes, hardware and overhead are included ([UMBC](https://userpages.cs.umbc.edu/olano/class/493-24-2/budgets.md.html)). The publisher rate is a fully loaded ceiling, not a wage, so it is shown for context only.

|---|---|---|---|---|

The Ismail column is the honest number to quote, because it uses the same benchmark that sets the stipend. Under the recommended tier, the founders cover about US$3,300 of their own pay against a modest global indie wage. The publisher column makes a different point: a publisher costing the same 12 weeks of two people would budget about **US$55,200**, more than three times the whole recommended cash ask. That comparison makes the US$17,200 ask look small without inflating it. Self-funded time is normal in this industry. GDC's 2025 survey found that **82% of indie developers have put their own money into their games** ([GDC 2025 State of the Game Industry](https://investgame.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/0794a269-d5c4-4994-9bcf-8c5730d0815e_2025_GDC_State_of_the_Game_Industry_report-1.pdf)).

## Self-funding and Kickstarter carry the budget, and most funds sit out of reach

Most of the funding landscape does not fit a US$1,000–27,000 ask from a two-person Godot team. **Epic MegaGrants** averages US$5,000–75,000, but it funds projects "built in and around Unreal Engine". Its only route for other engines is a reserved share for teams "switching to Unreal Engine" ([Epic MegaGrants](https://www.unrealengine.com/megagrants)), which makes no sense for a 12-week polish. Both 2026 cycles have closed, and no 2027 dates are published. The **Godot Foundation** spends donations on the engine, not on games ([Godot Foundation](https://godot.foundation/)). **Outersloth** says it will "most likely fund things between $50k-$2m" and will not consider released or Early Access games ([Innersloth](https://www.innersloth.com/outersloth)). The ask is below that floor, and the team would have to describe the itch.io build as a free prototype. Publisher advances in Voyer Law's 130+-deal sample start at **US$20,000**, with a **US$270,000 median** ([Legal Moves](https://legalmoveslawfirm.com/state-game-publishing-agreements)). Commentary from 2026 says "if you're releasing your first game, it's very hard to get any real money from publishers" ([SUPERJUMP](https://www.superjumpmagazine.com/state-of-indie-2026-part-3-finance-and-tech)). A no-advance marketing deal is more realistic. In 2025 developers kept a median **70%** of revenue on deals without an advance ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/voyer-law-publishing-agreements-in)). Akupara, which lists milestone-based development funding ([Akupara](https://www.akuparagames.com/publishing)), and Armor Games Studios ([Armor Games Studios](https://armorgamesstudios.com/)) are better genre fits than narrative labels.

Matched to the tiers, the plan is straightforward. **The lean tier (about US$900) is fully self-fundable**, which means the game ships whatever happens. **The recommended tier (about US$17,200) needs founders' money plus one outside source.** The founders can cover the US$6,580 of production costs and contingency, and a Kickstarter can fund the stipends. Fees take about 9% of a US$10,000 raise with 300 pledges, so a goal near **US$10,000** nets roughly the US$8,830 stipend line. Rewards should be digital, to avoid physical fulfilment costs. A campaign that funds the whole tier would need a goal of about US$19,000. In Kickstarter's own data for the Games category, which includes tabletop, 15% of funded projects raised US$10,000–19,999 and 44% raised US$1,000–9,999 ([Kickstarter Stats](https://web.archive.org/web/20260101190407/https://www.kickstarter.com/help/stats)). The smaller goal is the safer one. With a 32% video-game success rate, Kickstarter is upside rather than a base, and the stipend is the line that flexes if it fails. A 12-week schedule leaves little room for the usual pre-launch audience building plus a 30-day campaign, so the campaign either opens in the first weeks of the sprint or is replaced by pre-orders after the Steam page goes up. **The stretch tier (about US$26,500) has a realistic source only through WINGS**, if the team qualifies, or through an unusually strong Kickstarter. If neither applies, the stretch tier is a wish list and should stay out of the pitch.

The evidence is thinnest in three places. Freelance art and audio rates come from individual listings and vendor blogs, not from surveys with a stated method. Epic's FAQ answer on non-Unreal projects did not load, so the Godot exclusion rests on the page's main eligibility text. No source confirmed whether Antidote handles local two-player sessions, or whether BIC pays cash to international entrants.

## Conclusion

The pitch's numbers were never too big. They were unexplained. Itemising the pitch's own US$2,000 rate gives almost exactly its US$20,000 total, which shows the founders' instincts were sound and their presentation was the weak point. The more persuasive version is a little smaller, at US$17,200. Every line ties to a published rate, release costs are visible, and the stipend sits at a named benchmark's floor, with the founders' remaining time openly counted as their own stake. Dropping the US$60,000 and US$120,000 tiers removes nothing real. A bigger budget would mean a bigger project, such as a console port, pitched to a fund that writes cheques of that size.

The second implication concerns leverage. Because a US$900 release is fully self-fundable, the team can truthfully say the game ships either way, and that outside money buys polish and fair pay rather than survival. In a year when publishers say first games rarely get funded, that framing works with every audience the team can actually reach: Kickstarter backers, playtesters, festival juries and a possible no-advance publishing partner.
